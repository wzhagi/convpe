\begin{abstract}

ConvPE (Convolutional Position Embedding) improves Transformer performance and efficiency across language modeling, long-context extrapolation, and vision. On 3B tokens of FineWeb-Edu-10BT, it reduces final training perplexity by 8.41\% and raises the mean of eight zero-shot accuracy metrics by 2.35\% relative to a RoPE baseline, while using only 2.64\% more parameters and 2.71\% more forward-pass time. It matches the baseline's final loss with 31.3\% fewer training tokens and about 27\% less wall-clock training time. ConvPE also extrapolates better: on 1M training tokens it reduces perplexity by 71.1\% at 4K and 89.8\% at 8K contexts, and on RULER it raises average accuracy from 16.3\% to 21.1\% at 1K and from 3.0\% to 12.3\% at 2K. In vision, ConvPE2D improves CIFAR-100 accuracy by 10.9\% relative to RoPE. ConvPE realizes these gains with a windowed, content-dependent attention-score bias generated by a lightweight causal convolution and added directly to the scores. Because the bias is added without altering the queries or keys, ConvPE composes with RoPE, arbitrary masks, KV caching, and n-dimensional convolutions, and leaves positions outside the window unbiased. The code is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/wzhagi/convpe}.

\end{abstract}

Transformers~\cite{vaswani2017attention} are insensitive to input order, whereas word order is crucial for language understanding and the spatial layout of image patches matters for vision. Explicit or implicit position embeddings are therefore needed to inject order information into the model~\cite{devlin2019bert,radford2019language,su2021roformer}. Where this information is injected affects not only accuracy but also what the model can do at test time, especially when the context is longer than the training length.

Position information is currently injected at one of three points. Absolute and learnable embeddings add a position vector to the token representations~\cite{vaswani2017attention,devlin2019bert,radford2019language}; they are simple, but every position must be represented, and a learned table does not transfer to inputs longer than those seen in training. Rotary position embedding (RoPE)~\cite{su2021roformer} rotates queries and keys so that attention scores depend on relative distance; it is the default in many language models, and extending its context requires frequency rescaling or interpolation~\cite{peng2024yarn}. Attention-score-bias methods add a term directly to the score matrix: ALiBi~\cite{press2021train} uses a fixed linear decay, CABLE~\cite{veisi2025cable} learns to modulate a fixed distance-penalty skeleton, Composite~\cite{chang2021convolutionsselfattentionreinterpretingrelative} adds convolution-based relative-position terms, and DAPE~\cite{zheng2025dapev2} convolves the full score map, which is effective but adds another pass over a matrix that grows with the square of the context length.

These families thus couple the positional signal to the input representation, to the queries and keys, or to a distance prior on the scores. This paper takes a fourth route: leave the queries and keys unchanged, and generate a small positional bias locally. ConvPE projects each hidden state to $C$ channels and applies a causal convolution with window $W$ to produce $H_b\times W$ biases for the current query. The bias for relative offset $j$ is added to the score of the key $j$ positions back, for $j<W$; beyond the window, the score is unchanged. The module is content-dependent because its input is the current hidden state, and it is bounded because the convolution only sees the last $W$ tokens. Its cost is one $D\times C$ projection and one $W\times C\to W\times H_b$ convolution per layer. Because the bias is separate from $Q$ and $K$, ConvPE can be combined with RoPE, used with any attention mask, evaluated with a KV cache, and extended to two- or three-dimensional inputs by replacing the 1D convolution with a 2D or 3D one.

We evaluate ConvPE on language-model pretraining with 3B tokens of FineWeb-Edu-10BT, zero-shot evaluation on nine language tasks and twelve RULER tasks, and image classification with ViT-Tiny on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. \Cref{fig:overview} summarizes the relative changes with respect to the RoPE baseline: RULER and evaluation accuracies are ratios of the two models' average accuracies, and the token/time rows give the cost of reaching the baseline's final loss. \Cref{tab:pe,tab:valloss,tab:ruler,tab:cifar,tab:lm-summary,tab:cifar100} report the underlying numbers.

\textbf{Performance.}
ConvPE lowers the final training perplexity by 8.41\% and raises the mean of eight zero-shot accuracy metrics by 2.35\% relative to the RoPE baseline. On vision, ConvPE2D improves CIFAR-100 accuracy by 10.85\% relative (69.5\% vs 62.7\%).

\textbf{Training and token efficiency.}
ConvPE adds 2.64\% parameters and 2.71\% forward-pass time, and a training step becomes 6.5\% slower. It reaches the baseline's final loss with 31.3\% fewer training tokens, so the total wall-clock time needed to reach that loss falls by about 27\%.

\textbf{Context extension.}
On 1M training tokens, ConvPE reduces perplexity by 8.9\% at 1K, 15.3\% at 2K, 71.1\% at 4K, and 89.8\% at 8K contexts. On RULER, mean accuracy rises from 16.3\% to 21.1\% at 1K and from 3.0\% to 12.3\% at 2K.

\textbf{The main contributions of this paper are as follows:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose ConvPE, a convolutional attention-score-bias position embedding that combines strong performance with broad compatibility.
    \item We study the influence of ConvPE hyperparameters and architecture choices, identifying a simple default configuration with a favorable performance-efficiency balance.
    \item We demonstrate that ConvPE improves training efficiency, token efficiency, and zero-shot long-context extrapolation in language models.
    \item We verify the effectiveness of ConvPE2D in vision transformers on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100.
    \item We develop high-performance TileLang~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2504-17577} kernels for ConvPE.
\end{itemize}


\section{Background}

\subsection{Attention and Score Biases}

Given the input query $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{S_q \times D_k}$, key $K \in \mathbb{R}^{S_{kv} \times D_k}$, and value $V \in \mathbb{R}^{S_{kv} \times D_v}$, the computation of scaled dot-product attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} $O\in \mathbb{R}^{S_q \times D_v}$ is
\begin{equation}
O = \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{D_k}} + \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{M}\right)V,
\end{equation}
where $S_q$ is the query sequence length, $S_{kv}$ is the key/value sequence length, $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_q\times S_{kv}}$ is an additive attention-score bias, and $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_q\times S_{kv}}$ is a mask. A causal model sets $M_{ij}=-\infty$ for $j>i$, so that a query at position $s$ can attend only to keys at positions $t\le s$. With batch size and multi-head settings, $Q,K,V,O$ and the two bias terms carry additional batch and head dimensions; we omit them when they are unambiguous.

\subsection{Position Embeddings}

Existing position embedding schemes can be roughly divided into five categories:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Absolute Position Embedding}~\cite{vaswani2017attention}: adds a fixed positional vector, usually sine waves, to the input. It is simple to implement, but its performance is usually limited and it is not content-dependent.
    \item \textbf{Learnable Positional Bias}~\cite{devlin2019bert,radford2019language}: adds a learnable positional vector to the input sequence. It is typically used when the maximum length is fixed, such as in fixed-resolution images or text truncated to a fixed length.
    \item \textbf{Rotary Position Embedding}~\cite{su2021roformer}: encodes position as a rotation matrix and applies it to queries and keys, so that attention scores depend on relative positions. RoPE has been widely adopted in modern large language models, and it enables long-context extension through frequency scaling, such as YaRN~\cite{peng2024yarn}.
    \item \textbf{Attention Score Bias}: adds a positional bias $\mathbf{B}$ to attention scores~\cite{raffel2020exploring,press2021train,chang2021convolutionsselfattentionreinterpretingrelative}. RoPE can be viewed as a rotation-based equivalent, as it also makes attention scores depend on relative positions.
    \item \textbf{No Position Embedding (NoPE)}~\cite{kazemnejad2023impact}: adds no explicit position embeddings; instead, it relies on the model structure and causal attention masks to learn positional information implicitly. For example, Kimi K3 applies NoPE to its global-attention layers~\cite{kimi2026k3}.
\end{enumerate}

ConvPE belongs to the fourth category, but differs from existing score-bias methods in two respects. First, the bias is produced by a bounded causal convolution over the hidden states, rather than by a fixed distance function or by a convolution over the full score matrix. Second, the bias is applied only to the $W$ most recent keys; beyond the window, the bias is exactly zero, so the model falls back to content-based attention. \Cref{sec:method} makes this precise.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Windowed Convolutional Score Bias}

Given token representations $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times S\times D}$, ConvPE first projects them to a small channel dimension,
\begin{equation}
X_{\mathrm{down}} = X W_p, \qquad W_p\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times C},
\end{equation}
and then applies a causal convolution with window $W$,
\begin{equation}
O_{\mathrm{Conv}} = \mathrm{CausalConv1d}\left(X_{\mathrm{down}};\, W,\, C\to H_bW\right),
\end{equation}
where $O_{\mathrm{Conv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times S\times H_bW}$ and the convolution is left-padded by $W-1$ positions. The output is reshaped into the score bias
$\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{ConvPE}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times S\times H_b\times W}$. For a query at position $s$ and a key at position $t\le s$, the bias added to the attention score of head $h$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}_{n,h,s,t}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ConvPE}}_{n,s,\,h\bmod H_b,\,s-t}, & 0\le s-t<W,\\[2pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the batch size, $H$ is the number of attention heads, and $H_b$ must divide $H$. In words, head $h$ reads its bias from bias head $h\bmod H_b$, so each bias head is shared by $H/H_b$ attention heads. Each bias head therefore learns a different relative-position pattern. Future keys are removed by the causal mask, and keys older than $W$ are scored without a positional bias. \Cref{fig:convpe_method} illustrates the module and the resulting bias pattern.

The hyperparameters are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $W$: convolution window size.
    \item $C$: output dimension of the linear projection and input channels of the convolution.
    \item $H_b$: number of score-bias heads.
\end{itemize}

The projection has $DC$ parameters and the convolution has $C H_b W^2$ parameters. Each parameter is used once per token, so the multiply--accumulate count per token is the same. Together with the $H_bW$ bias terms, a layer has $DC + C H_b W^2 + H_b W$ parameters; for the default configuration this is $3.29$M over 12 layers, which accounts for the $+2.64\%$ parameter increase reported in \Cref{sec:pretrain}.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Variants}

To reduce the convolution parameter count, we introduce three additional hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{Groups}$: number of convolution groups, which split the $C$ input channels and the $H_bW$ output channels into $G$ groups. Each group has its own kernel, and the parameter count becomes $C H_b W^2/G$.
    \item $\mathrm{Share}$: whether to share one kernel across all groups. Sharing divides the convolution parameter count by another factor of $G$ (for $G=1$ it has no effect).
    \item $\mathrm{Rank}$: rank $R$ of a low-rank factorization of the convolution. A group-shared down-projection maps the $(C/G)W$ input window to $R$ dimensions, and a group-wise up-projection maps $R$ back to the $H_bW$ outputs. This costs $(C/G)WR + R H_b W$ weight parameters instead of $C H_b W^2/G$, plus the same $H_bW$ bias term.
\end{itemize}
The default configuration uses $G=1$, no sharing, and $R=0$ (no factorization).

\subsection{Properties and Scope}

\textbf{Compatibility.} ConvPE only adds a bias to the attention scores, so it can be combined with RoPE applied to queries and keys, and with any mask (causal, document, sliding-window, or padding) because the mask is applied separately. For autoregressive decoding, the convolution needs only the projected states of the last $W-1$ tokens; storing them next to the KV cache costs $C(W-1)$ values per layer, independent of the context length. Replacing the 1D convolution with an $n$-dimensional convolution and indexing the corresponding neighborhood yields ConvPE2D, ConvPE3D, and so on.

\textbf{Scope.} The bias is exactly zero outside the window. ConvPE therefore imposes no positional prior beyond $W$ tokens, and in that region the model behaves like NoPE and must rely on content. This is a deliberate locality assumption: we do not claim that position is irrelevant at long range, only that a learned local bias is sufficient to improve the tasks we study. \Cref{sec:eval} tests whether this behavior extrapolates beyond the training length.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Architecture.} Unless stated otherwise, all language models have $N_{\mathrm{layers}}=12$ and use pre-RMSNorm, SwiGLU~\cite{shazeer2020glu} feed-forward layers with $8\times D_{\mathrm{model}}^2$ parameters, multi-query attention (MQA)~\cite{shazeer2019fast} with a single key/value head ($K=V$: one projection feeds both keys and values~\cite{deepseek2026v4}), query L2 normalization, key RMSNorm, a learnable attention sink per head, and tied input/output embeddings. Each attention layer has $D_{\mathrm{model}}\times D_{\mathrm{head}}\times(2N_{\mathrm{heads}}+1)$ parameters, excluding normalization scales and the per-head sink. The tiny backbone has $D_{\mathrm{model}}=192$, $N_{\mathrm{heads}}=3$, $D_{\mathrm{head}}=64$, and the base backbone has $D_{\mathrm{model}}=768$, $N_{\mathrm{heads}}=12$, $D_{\mathrm{head}}=128$ (so the query and output projections are $1536$-dimensional). The RoPE baseline uses a rotary dimension of $64$ and base $10000$. The vocabulary has $50{,}304$ entries (the GPT-2 tokenizer~\cite{radford2019language} padded to a multiple of 128).

\textbf{Data.} Pretraining uses the first 3B tokens of FineWeb-Edu-10BT~\cite{penedo2024fineweb}, packed into 1024-token blocks. The hyperparameter search trains for one epoch on WikiText-103~\cite{merity2016pointer} and reports the held-out loss on the WikiText-2 test split.

\textbf{Optimization.} For pretraining, Muon~\cite{jordan2024muon} optimizes the hidden 2D parameters at a learning rate of $3\times10^{-3}$ with momentum $0.95$ and weight decay $0.01$, while Adam~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/KingmaB14} optimizes the remaining parameters at $3\times10^{-4}$. Both use a cosine schedule with 500 warm-up steps. We use a per-device batch size of $4$ with $4$ gradient-accumulation steps ($16$ sequences, $16{,}384$ tokens per step), sequence length $1024$, BF16 autocast, gradient clipping at $4.0$, $183{,}204$ steps (3.0B tokens), and seed $42$. The language models are trained with the Hugging Face Trainer~\cite{wolf2020transformers}. The hyperparameter search uses AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} at $3\times10^{-4}$ with weight decay $0.01$, a cosine schedule, per-device batch size $4$, sequence length $1024$, one epoch, BF16, gradient clipping at $1.0$, and seed $42$. All language-model evaluations are zero-shot. All language-model results come from a single run; small task-level differences should be read with sampling noise in mind.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Search}
\label{sec:hp}

We train 10 tiny and 8 base configurations and select by held-out loss after one epoch. Model names record the backbone (\texttt{tiny} or \texttt{base}), the position mechanism (\texttt{rope} or \texttt{nope}), and the ConvPE fields $W$, $C$, $R$, $H_b$, and $G$ as \texttt{w}, \texttt{d}, \texttt{r}, \texttt{h}, and \texttt{g} followed by their values, with a trailing \texttt{s} when the convolution kernel is shared across groups. For example, \texttt{tiny rope w128d6r128h3g1s} denotes a tiny backbone with RoPE, $W=128$, $C=6$, $R=128$, $H_b=3$, $G=1$, and weight sharing. Fields equal to the default (position mechanism \texttt{nope}, $R=0$, $H_b=1$, $G=1$, and no sharing) are omitted when this is unambiguous, e.g., \texttt{base w128d16} is \texttt{base nope w128d16r0h1g1}.

First, we train tiny models around \texttt{nope w128d6r0h3g1}. \Cref{fig:wt103t} shows that every ConvPE variant outperforms the RoPE baseline ($4.249$ vs $3.942$--$4.073$). Increasing the projection dimension from $6$ to $12$ ($3.966\to3.942$) and the window from $64$ to $128$ ($4.047\to3.966$) give the largest gains. Grouped convolution hurts the most ($4.048$ for \texttt{g3} and $4.073$ for \texttt{g3s}), and a smaller window costs about as much ($4.047$). The rank-128 variant ($3.955$), RoPE integration ($3.962$), and the full kernel ($3.966$) are close, whereas $H_b=1$ is $0.011$ worse in this sweep ($3.977$).

We then train base models around \texttt{nope w128d4r0h4g1} and \texttt{nope w128d16r0h1g1}. \Cref{fig:wt103b} shows that every ConvPE variant again beats the RoPE baseline ($3.986$). The best configuration is \texttt{w128d16r0h1g1} ($3.489$), followed by the RoPE-integrated variant ($3.554$), \texttt{w128d4r0h1g1} ($3.563$), \texttt{w128d8r128h4} ($3.564$), \texttt{w128d4r0h4} ($3.566$), \texttt{w128d16g4s} ($3.574$), and \texttt{w128d4r128h4} ($3.589$). Because $H_b=1$ is also the cheapest bias-head setting, we adopt \texttt{w128d16r0h1g1} as the default.

We also tried feeding $Q$ (as in Composite~\cite{chang2021convolutionsselfattentionreinterpretingrelative}), $KV$, or $X$ directly to the convolution; all three performed worse than first projecting $X$ to the low-dimensional $X_{\mathrm{down}}$.

\subsection{Pretraining}
\label{sec:pretrain}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]{pretrain.png}
    \caption{Training loss on the first 3B tokens of FineWeb-Edu-10BT for the RoPE baseline and ConvPE \texttt{w128d16}. The dashed curve is the loss difference (RoPE minus ConvPE).}
    \label{fig:pretrain}
\end{figure}

We train the RoPE baseline and ConvPE \texttt{w128d16} from scratch on the first 3B tokens of FineWeb-Edu-10BT with the recipe in \Cref{sec:setup}. \Cref{fig:pretrain} shows that ConvPE converges faster and reaches a lower final loss. Averaged over the last 0.1B tokens, the final training loss drops from $3.1195$ to $3.0316$ ($-2.82\%$ in loss, corresponding to perplexity $22.64\to20.73$, or $-8.41\%$). Interpolating the smoothed training curves, ConvPE reaches the baseline's final loss after $2.06$B tokens instead of $3$B ($31.3\%$ fewer tokens).

To measure cost, we implement high-performance Flash MQA kernels in TileLang and benchmark the two configurations on one GPU with batch size $4$ and sequence length $1024$, averaged over $1{,}000$ iterations after $1{,}000$ warm-up iterations. ConvPE has $128.13$M parameters versus $124.84$M ($+2.64\%$), and its forward pass takes $23.14$ ms versus $22.53$ ms ($+2.71\%$). During training, ConvPE processes $2.60$ iterations/s versus $2.77$ ($6.5\%$ more time per step). The per-step overhead is larger than the forward-pass overhead, which is consistent with the ConvPE backward kernel being less optimized than its forward kernel. Combining the $31.3\%$ token reduction with the $6.5\%$ per-step overhead gives a net reduction of about $27\%$ in the wall-clock time needed to match the baseline's loss. The exact value depends on how the curves are smoothed, so we report it as approximate.

\subsection{Attention Analysis}
\label{sec:attn_analysis}

To understand the role of the ConvPE module, we analyze the kernel weights and the static output bias of the trained \texttt{base w128d16} model. Since the average weights are close to zero, the standard deviation indicates \textbf{feature selectivity}: a higher standard deviation means that the kernel focuses on specific channels. \Cref{fig:attn} shows three properties.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{attention.png}
    \caption{ConvPE statistics of the pretrained \texttt{base w128d16} model across layers. Left: standard deviation of the kernel weights in the input dimension. Middle: standard deviation in the output dimension. Right: static output bias, averaged over bias heads. The x-axis is the relative offset ($-127$ to $0$; $0$ is the current token).}
    \label{fig:attn}
\end{figure}

\textbf{1. Near-end dominance.}
Near the current token, both the static bias and the input-feature selectivity reach their largest magnitudes. This indicates that the positional relationship between nearby tokens is important: ConvPE applies highly specific feature extraction and strong positional priors to the immediate local context.

\textbf{2. Far-end relaxation.}
Toward the far end of the window, the standard deviation is lower and the static bias is close to zero for most layers. ConvPE therefore does not impose a strong static prior at this range, leaving the decision to the convolution over the dynamic input and to the query--key semantic match.

\textbf{3. Self-suppression and self-enhancement.}
The static bias has a clear layer-wise pattern at the current position. Layer 0 shows self-enhancement with a bias of $+0.5885$, whereas every deeper layer except L4 (a near-zero $+0.0115$) shows self-suppression with a negative bias. This is consistent with deeper layers shifting attention away from the current token and toward the historical context.

\Cref{tab:lm-summary} shows that ConvPE is in the first tier on both the perplexity diagnostic and the accuracy metrics. At 1K it has the second-lowest PPL ($20.52$) after DAPE V2 ($19.47$), and at 2K it is again second ($19.95$ vs $18.71$). At 4K and 8K, DAPE V2, CABLE, and ALiBi extrapolate better on this diagnostic: ConvPE reaches $20.67$ and $29.33$, respectively, while ALiBi stays near $20.5$. ConvPE is nonetheless far better than NoPE, Composite, and RoPE at long contexts (e.g., at 4K, $20.67$ vs $55.01$ for RoPE and $246.16$ for Composite; at 8K, $29.33$ vs $207.58$ for RoPE). On RULER, ConvPE has the best mean accuracy at 1K ($26.58\%$), is second at 2K ($14.46\%$) and 4K ($3.49\%$), and is never below second place. Its downstream accuracy ($44.66\%$) is within $0.08$ points of the best result ($44.74\%$). DAPE V2 convolves the full score map, so it is $1.84\times$ slower per step than RoPE ($273.71$ vs $149.03$ ms) and uses $20.25$ vs $10.77$ GiB of training memory. ALiBi~\cite{press2021train} and CABLE~\cite{veisi2025cable} are cheaper, but both retain a distance-penalty form. ALiBi applies a fixed linear penalty, and CABLE learns to modulate a distance-penalty skeleton. In both cases the bias contains a component that grows with the offset, so distant keys are penalized; CABLE can adjust the magnitude with content, but the distance component remains. This prior is reasonable for local language modeling, but it conflicts with long-context retrieval, where the relevant evidence may lie far from the query. Prior work supports this concern: Kazemnejad et al.~\cite{kazemnejad2023impact} find that ALiBi and other explicit position encodings are not well suited for length generalization on downstream tasks, whereas NoPE, which imposes no distance prior, generalizes better. RULER exhibits the same pattern in our reproduction: ConvPE outperforms both ALiBi and CABLE at every length ($26.58\%$ vs $14.37\%$ and $19.80\%$ at 1K; $14.46\%$ vs $3.80\%$ and $7.69\%$ at 2K; $3.49\%$ vs $1.61\%$ and $3.22\%$ at 4K). Because ConvPE applies no bias outside the window, attention beyond $W$ remains content-based, so distant evidence is not suppressed by construction. The trade-off is visible in the 8K language-modeling diagnostic, where ALiBi and CABLE are better (PPL $20.52$ and $19.52$ vs $29.33$); this suggests that a distance penalty can help next-token prediction by smoothing attention even as it hurts retrieval. For long-context tasks that require retrieving distant evidence, we consider the RULER behavior the more relevant one. This comparison covers one scale ($163$--$167$M parameters), so we do not claim that the ordering transfers to larger models.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

ConvPE adds a learned, windowed bias to attention scores without altering the queries or keys. In our experiments it improves pretraining loss, zero-shot accuracy, context extension, and CIFAR accuracy at a $2.6\%$ parameter and $2.7\%$ forward-pass cost. The independent reproduction shows that ConvPE's 8K perplexity is higher than that of ALiBi, CABLE, and DAPE V2, but this does not make those methods better suited to long-context training. ConvPE outperforms ALiBi and CABLE on RULER at every length, and its average downstream accuracy is within $0.08$ points of the best result. DAPE V2 is stronger on the 8K perplexity diagnostic and on RULER at 2K and 4K, but it must materialize and convolve the full score matrix: it is quadratic in context length, incompatible with fused FlashAttention kernels, and therefore impractical for long-context training. ConvPE is the only method among the four that combines competitive downstream accuracy with a FlashAttention-compatible design that has no fixed distance penalty; when accuracy, parameter count, per-step cost, and compatibility are considered jointly, it is the most favorable choice for long-context training.

We adopt $H_b=1$ without any parameter-saving technique as the default configuration; this configuration achieved the best held-out loss in the base-model sweep and is also the cheapest bias-head setting. ConvPE is compatible with RoPE, various attention masks, and KV-cache concatenation, and it naturally supports multidimensional extensions.

Our study has three main limitations. First, the main experiments use a single seed and models of up to $167$M parameters, so the size of the gains at larger scale is unknown. Second, the window is a hard locality assumption: beyond $W$ tokens the model receives no positional bias, which is beneficial for extrapolation but may hurt tasks that require precise long-range positions. Third, the 8K loss diagnostic uses training tokens rather than held-out text. We view these as directions for future work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kernel optimization.} Further optimize the backward and decode kernels.
    \item \textbf{Scaling behavior.} Investigate ConvPE with larger Transformers and datasets, multiple seeds, and identify configuration patterns at scale.
    \item \textbf{Long-context modeling.} Evaluate ConvPE at contexts of $32$K tokens and beyond, and with long-context training rather than zero-shot extension.
    \item \textbf{Architectural integration.} Combine ConvPE with Sliding Window Attention (SWA)~\cite{beltagy2020longformer}, Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA)~\cite{deepseek2026v4}, and other Transformer variants.
    \item \textbf{Higher-dimensional modalities.} Extend ConvPE to video, volumetric data, and multimodal inputs with 3D and higher-dimensional convolutions.
\end{itemize}
